## SECTION 1 — INTRODUCTION
*~350 words · 3 paragraphs*

At the start of the twentieth century, an estimated 100,000 wild tigers
roamed Asia. By 1973, fewer than 1,800 remained in India alone — and the
trajectory was unmistakably downward. Poaching, habitat loss, and the
collapse of prey populations had driven one of the world's most iconic
predators to the edge of extinction within a single human lifetime. That
year, the Indian government launched Project Tiger: a coordinated national
program to protect core habitats, relocate villages from reserve interiors,
and invest in the forest department infrastructure needed to enforce
protection at scale. What followed is one of the most consequential wildlife
conservation interventions ever attempted.

India's National Tiger Conservation Authority has conducted systematic
population assessments every four years since 2006, using camera trap
capture-recapture analysis — the Spatially Explicit Capture-Recapture (SECR)
method — to generate statistically rigorous population estimates across all
54 tiger reserves. Five census rounds spanning 2006 to 2022 have documented
a national population growing from approximately 1,411 tigers to 3,682: a
161% increase in sixteen years. These are estimates, not exact counts, and
each carries a confidence interval. But the directional signal across five
independent assessments is unambiguous. India's tigers are recovering.

This project examines seven reserves that exemplify that recovery. Spread
across five Indian states and five distinct ecological landscapes — from the
Brahmaputra floodplains of Assam to the Aravalli Hills of Rajasthan, from
the Western Ghats rainforests of Karnataka to the Himalayan foothills of
Uttarakhand — these reserves represent the geographic and ecological breadth
of India's tiger conservation success. They do not all tell the same story.
Some have tripled their populations in sixteen years. Others have held steady
at densities that approach the ecological limits of their habitat. What unites
them is that they have all, in their own way, worked. The maps and analysis
that follow explore how, and why.

## SECTION 2 — THE OVERVIEW MAP
*~200 words · 2 paragraphs*

**[Embed: Web Map 1 — Tiger Conservation Reserve Overview]**

The map above shows all seven featured reserves, colored by population growth
category. Dark green reserves — Pench, Bandipur, Nagarahole, and Ranthambore
— achieved greater than 75% population growth between 2006 and 2022. Medium
green marks Jim Corbett, with moderate but sustained growth from an already
large base. Olive marks Kanha, stable and productive over the full sixteen
years. Gold marks Kaziranga, a reserve operating not at a growth trajectory
but at carrying capacity — the ecological ceiling for its landscape.

What the map also communicates, simply by showing where these reserves sit,
is the diversity of India's tiger conservation challenge. No two of these
reserves share a state, a landscape type, or a primary threat profile. Pench
straddles a state boundary in the Satpura Hills. Kaziranga floods every
monsoon as the Brahmaputra overflows its banks. Ranthambore shares a fence
line with a busy town. Bandipur is bisected by a national highway. That
tigers are recovering in all of these places simultaneously is not an
accident — it is the result of sustained, systematic protection applied
consistently across very different conditions.

## SECTION 3 — THE NUMBERS
*~300 words · 3 paragraphs*

**[Embed: Chart 1 — Tiger Population Growth Across Seven Reserves, 2006–2022]**

The trend lines above tell seven stories simultaneously. Jim Corbett traces
the steadiest upward arc — beginning 2006 with 164 tigers, the highest
starting population of the group, and climbing consistently to 260 by 2022.
Pench begins at just 33 tigers and ends at 77: a 133% increase that, in
percentage terms, outpaces every other reserve in the analysis. Kaziranga's
line is almost flat — 103 tigers in 2010, 104 in 2022 — but this should be
read as ecological success, not stagnation. A reserve sustaining roughly 100
tigers across twelve years in a habitat bounded by a major river and a
mountain range is operating at the upper limit of what that landscape can
support. Ranthambore's line plateaus after 2018 at 57 tigers, suggesting a
similar dynamic: not failure, but a population that has reached the carrying
capacity of its semi-arid terrain.

**[Embed: Chart 2 — Percent Population Change, 2006–2022]**

Ranked by total percentage growth, Pench leads at 133%, followed by Bandipur
at 121%. But percentage growth and absolute growth tell different stories:
Jim Corbett, ranked fifth at 58.5%, added 96 tigers in absolute terms — more
than any other reserve. Kanha, at 18%, added only 16 tigers — but it was
already maintaining one of the largest populations in Central India.
Understanding both metrics together is essential: the reserves that grew
fastest from a small base and the reserves that sustained large populations
from a large base represent equally important conservation achievements.

All figures are NTCA statistical estimates derived from camera trap SECR
analysis. Confidence intervals are available in the original census reports;
this analysis uses point estimates (midpoints) throughout for consistency
across census years.

## SECTION 4 — WHERE TIGERS ARE DETECTED
*~350 words · 4 paragraphs*

**[Embed: Web Map 2a — KDE Baseline 2006–2010]**
**[Embed: Web Map 2b — KDE Current 2018–2022]**

The two maps above show kernel density estimates of tiger occurrence records
from the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF): a global repository
of georeferenced wildlife observations contributed by researchers,
institutions, and citizen scientists. Darker green indicates higher
concentrations of tiger detections. Both maps use identical classification
breaks, making them directly comparable — a darker area in 2018–2022 than in
2006–2010 at the same location represents a genuine increase in detection
density, not a rescaling artifact.

The contrast between the two periods is visible across all seven reserves.
The 2006–2010 baseline shows sparse, tight kernels — reflecting both a lower
tiger population and the relatively limited number of GBIF records from that
period (116 occurrence points after filtering). The 2018–2022 current period
shows noticeably larger and more diffuse green areas, particularly at
Corbett, Kanha, and Kaziranga, consistent with expanded detection effort
accompanying a growing population.

**[Embed: Web Map 3 — Hot Spot Analysis (Getis-Ord Gi*)]**

The hot spot map applies Getis-Ord Gi* spatial statistics to identify
locations where tiger detections cluster more than would be expected by
chance. Orange and red points indicate statistically significant hot spots
— areas of concentrated detection at 90%, 95%, and 99% confidence levels.
Purple indicates cold spots: areas where detections are sparser than the
surrounding average. Confirmed hot spot clusters appear in the Kanha-Pench
corridor of Central India and near Kaziranga in the Northeast.

A critical analytical note: GBIF records reflect where people look, not
just where tigers are. Researchers and tourists tend to concentrate near
park gates, roads, and established safari zones — the accessible periphery
of reserves rather than their interiors. This observer effort bias means
that cold spots and not-significant results in this analysis do not indicate
an absence of tigers; they indicate an absence of *observers*. The next
section illustrates this most clearly.

## SECTION 5 — RESERVE PROFILES

---

### 5a. Ranthambore Tiger Reserve
*~280 words · Rajasthan · Semi-Arid · High growth 78%*

**[Reserve photo: Ranthambore]**

**[Embed: Reserve — Ranthambore Tiger Reserve map]**

**Stat card:**
2006 Population: 32 · 2022 Population: 57 · Growth: +78% · Tigers/100 km²: 3.23 · Forest Cover: 77% · Landscape: Semi-Arid Aravalli

Ranthambore is the reserve that should not work — and does. In a state where
forests cover less than 5% of the land, where the nearest town sits within
walking distance of the park boundary, and where the terrain is rocky,
seasonally dry, and bounded by agricultural land on three sides, Ranthambore
has grown its tiger population from 32 to 57 over sixteen years. It is proof
that tigers are more adaptable than their reputation as deep-forest predators
suggests — provided the core habitat is protected and prey populations are
maintained.

The Aravalli Hills that form Ranthambore's spine are ancient and dramatic:
ridgelines of bare quartzite rising above dry deciduous forest, punctuated by
seasonal lakes that concentrate prey during summer. The ruins of Ranthambore
Fort, a tenth-century structure that sits inside the reserve, make this one
of the most visually distinctive tiger habitats in the world. The tigers here
have been among the most photographed in India — a fact that reflects both
the reserve's accessibility from Sawai Madhopur and the relative openness of
its terrain.

That accessibility creates an analytical artifact worth understanding
directly: the dense cluster of Cold Spot detections visible north of the
reserve in the hot spot map reflects the concentration of tourist and
researcher observations near the park gate at Sawai Madhopur — not an
absence of tigers. The census data tells a different story. Ranthambore's
plateau from 2018 to 2022 at 57 tigers most likely reflects a population
approaching the carrying capacity of its current protected area, with
individuals dispersing into the surrounding Rajasthan landscape.

### 5b. Pench Tiger Reserve
*~270 words · Madhya Pradesh / Maharashtra · Central Indian Sal Forest · High growth 133%*

**[Reserve photo: Pench]**

**[Embed: Reserve — Pench Tiger Reserve map]**

**Stat card:**
2006 Population: 33 · 2022 Population: 77 · Growth: +133% · Tigers/100 km²: 4.04 · Forest Cover: ~89% · Landscape: Central Indian Sal-Teak Forest

No reserve in this analysis grew faster than Pench. Starting from just 33
tigers in 2006 — the smallest baseline population of the seven — Pench more
than doubled to 77 by 2022, achieving the highest percentage growth of any
featured reserve. That trajectory reflects the compounding effect of strong
anti-poaching enforcement, robust prey recovery, and the ecological
connectivity that links Pench to its neighbor Kanha through forested corridors
in the Satpura Hills.

Pench is treated in this analysis as a single combined landscape spanning
Madhya Pradesh and Maharashtra, because the NTCA census surveys the tiger
population across the full trans-boundary area rather than by state. The
reserve straddles the Satpura plateau, a mosaic of sal and teak forest
drained by the Pench River — the reserve's ecological spine, visible
threading through the center of the reserve in the map above. The hot spot
analysis confirms a statistically significant cluster of detections in the
broader Kanha-Pench corridor, underscoring the functional connectivity
between these two reserves.

Pench is also the reserve that inspired Rudyard Kipling's *The Jungle Book*:
the forests of Seeonee, where Mowgli was raised by wolves, are these very
sal forests. That the landscape now holds its highest tiger density in
decades is a conservation story with a satisfying literary resonance.

### 5c. Bandipur & Nagarahole National Parks
*~290 words · Karnataka · Western Ghats · High growth 121% / 81%*

**[Reserve photo: Bandipur or Nagarahole — Western Ghats landscape]**

**[Embed: Reserve — Bandipur & Nagarahole National Parks map]**

**Stat card:**
Bandipur — 2006: 68 · 2022: 150 · Growth: +121% · Forest Cover: 84%
Nagarahole — 2006: 78 · 2022: 141 · Growth: +81% · Forest Cover: 84% · Landscape: Western Ghats Tropical Forest

In the Nilgiri Biosphere Reserve of southern Karnataka, two national parks
share a boundary and, effectively, a tiger population. Bandipur and
Nagarahole together form one of India's most productive tiger landscapes —
nearly 3,000 square kilometers of contiguous Western Ghats forest where
tigers, elephants, gaur, and leopards coexist at densities rarely seen
elsewhere in the country. Bandipur grew from 68 tigers in 2006 to 150 in
2022; Nagarahole from 78 to 141. Together, the complex added 145 tigers over
sixteen years.

The terrain here is layered: the Western Ghats escarpment rises steeply to
the west toward Kerala, the Nilgiri Hills climb to over 2,200 meters to the
south toward Ooty, and the Kabini Reservoir — formed by the damming of the
Kabini River — spreads across the interior of Nagarahole, drawing wildlife
to its banks year-round. The map above captures this geography clearly:
the dark ridgelines of the Ghats, the blue water body of the Kabini, and
the connected reserve polygon spanning both parks.

What the map also shows, running as a thin gray line through Bandipur, is
National Highway 181 — the Mysore-Ooty road. This highway, which carries
substantial truck and tourist traffic through the core of the reserve,
has been the subject of a long-running legal battle over nighttime traffic
restrictions. That Bandipur has achieved 121% tiger growth despite this
persistent corridor fragmentation speaks to the underlying productivity
of the Western Ghats habitat and the effectiveness of Karnataka's forest
management.

### 5d. Kanha National Park
*~260 words · Madhya Pradesh · Central India · Stable 18%*

**[Reserve photo: Kanha meadows]**

**[Embed: Reserve — Kanha National Park map]**

**Stat card:**
2006 Population: 89 · 2022 Population: 105 · Growth: +18% · Tigers/100 km²: 5.07 · Forest Cover: 91% · Landscape: Central Indian Sal-Bamboo Forest

Not every conservation success story is a growth story. Kanha National Park
has maintained a population of 89 to 105 tigers across the full sixteen-year
period — the most stable trajectory in this analysis, and arguably the most
impressive. Sustaining a large predator population at near-constant levels in
a reserve of 2,072 square kilometers, surrounded by one of India's most
densely populated rural regions, requires the kind of consistent, unglamorous
institutional effort that rarely makes headlines.

Kanha's two-lobe shape — visible clearly in the map above — reflects the
reserve's ecology: a core zone of mature sal forest in the western lobe,
connected by a corridor to the buffer zone in the eastern lobe, where the
transition to more open bamboo forest supports different prey assemblages.
This landscape heterogeneity within a single reserve is part of what makes
Kanha productive across decades: different habitat types support different
prey species, distributing the tiger population's foraging pressure and
reducing competition.

Kanha is also historically significant as one of India's first Project Tiger
reserves, designated in 1973. The relocation of Baiga tribal villages from
the core zone in the 1970s — a decision whose human costs were substantial
and whose legacy remains contested — created the inviolate core that
underlies the reserve's ecological productivity today. That history is
inseparable from the conservation outcome the data shows.

### 5e. Kaziranga National Park
*~270 words · Assam · Northeast India · Stable at carrying capacity*

**[Reserve photo: Kaziranga — Brahmaputra floodplain]**

**[Embed: Reserve — Kaziranga National Park map]**

**Stat card:**
2010 Population: 103 · 2022 Population: 104 · Density: ~8.8 tigers/100 km² · Forest Cover: 86% · Landscape: Brahmaputra Alluvial Floodplain

No 2006 reserve-level estimate is available for Kaziranga — it is excluded
from the percentage growth ranking for this reason. But its story belongs in
this analysis for a different metric entirely: density. At approximately 8.8
tigers per 100 square kilometers by census estimates — and likely higher
within the core zone — Kaziranga supports the highest tiger density of any
reserve in this project, and among the highest in the world.

The map above explains why. Kaziranga sits on a narrow strip of alluvial
floodplain between the Brahmaputra River to the north and the Karbi Anglong
Hills to the south — a natural enclosure that has, for millennia, concentrated
prey at exceptional densities. Indian one-horned rhinoceroses, water buffalo,
swamp deer, and wild pig inhabit the grasslands and wetlands in numbers
that sustain a large tiger population within a relatively compact area of
1,180 square kilometers. Every monsoon, the Brahmaputra floods much of
the reserve, pushing tigers and other wildlife up into the Karbi Anglong
Hills — a seasonal movement that makes corridor protection between the
reserve and the hills a conservation priority.

The nearly flat census line from 2010 to 2022 is not evidence of stagnation.
It is a reserve at ecological equilibrium: producing tigers at the rate
the landscape can sustain, with individuals dispersing into the broader
Northeast India tiger landscape. Kaziranga is not a reserve that needs
to grow — it is a reserve that needs to be maintained.

### 5f. Jim Corbett National Park
*~280 words · Uttarakhand · Terai Arc · Moderate growth 58%*

**[Reserve photo: Jim Corbett — Ramganga valley or Himalayan foothills]**

**[Embed: Reserve — Jim Corbett National Park map]**

**Stat card:**
2006 Population: 164 · 2022 Population: 260 · Growth: +58% · Forest Cover: 44%* · Landscape: Himalayan Terai-Bhabar Forest

*Forest cover figure requires verification — likely reflects a boundary
revision between 2011 and 2021 ISFR assessments rather than actual forest loss.

Jim Corbett National Park holds more tigers than any other reserve in this
analysis — 260 by the 2022 census — and has done so from the largest starting
point as well. Growing from 164 to 260 tigers over sixteen years represents
a 58% increase and an addition of 96 individuals: more tigers added in
absolute terms than any other featured reserve. For a reserve that was already
operating at high density in 2006, sustaining that trajectory for sixteen
years is a significant achievement.

Corbett's terrain is the most dramatic of the seven reserves: the Ramganga
Reservoir sits at roughly 400 meters elevation in the valley floor, while
the surrounding Himalayan foothills rise to nearly 2,000 meters within the
reserve boundary. This elevation gradient — from Terai grasslands at the
base to Himalayan oak-rhododendron forests on the ridges — creates a
diversity of habitat types that supports tigers, leopards, elephants, and
a rich assemblage of deer and wild pig prey species simultaneously.

The reserve takes its name from Jim Corbett, the hunter-turned-conservationist
who spent decades in these forests tracking and killing man-eating tigers and
leopards, and whose later writing helped build public support for wildlife
protection in colonial and early post-independence India. That the forests
he documented now hold their highest tiger density in a century is a fitting
conclusion to his conservation legacy.

## SECTION 6 — WHAT THE DATA TELLS US
*~300 words · 3 paragraphs*

**[Embed: Chart 3 — Tigers per 100 km², 2006 vs 2022]**

Population counts and growth rates tell part of the story; density completes
it. The chart above compares tigers per 100 square kilometers in 2006 and
2022 for each featured reserve. Nagarahole leads on density in 2022 at 12.23
tigers per 100 km², followed by Jim Corbett at 17.78 — though Corbett's
figure should be interpreted cautiously, as the KBA boundary used in this
analysis is smaller than the legal Tiger Reserve boundary, likely inflating
the density calculation. What the chart shows unambiguously is that every
reserve has increased its density over sixteen years, and that the increases
are not uniform: Pench and Bandipur show the most dramatic per-unit-area
improvements, while Kanha and Ranthambore show more modest density gains
from an already productive baseline.

**[Embed: Chart 4 — Reserve Area vs 2022 Population, sized by Growth Rate]**

The bubble chart above plots each reserve by its area (x-axis) against its
2022 population (y-axis), with bubble size representing percentage growth.
The key finding is in what the chart does not show: no clear relationship
between reserve area and tiger population or growth rate. Kanha, the largest
reserve at 2,072 km², has a smaller 2022 population than Corbett, which is
400 km² smaller. Pench, a combined landscape of roughly 1,907 km², achieved
the highest growth rate of any reserve. What the analysis cannot fully
quantify — but what the literature consistently confirms — is that the
variables that most strongly predict tiger recovery are management intensity,
prey biomass, and connectivity to other protected areas: factors that operate
at the scale of the reserve but are not captured by area alone.

Across all seven reserves, a consistent set of enabling conditions appears:
effective anti-poaching enforcement, adequate prey populations sustained
through habitat management, and the systematic accountability provided by
NTCA's four-year census cycle. The census data does more than document
recovery — it creates the evidence base that drives management decisions,
focuses resources on reserves showing early decline, and demonstrates to
policymakers and the public that conservation investment produces measurable
results.

## SECTION 7 — CONCLUSION & CREDITS

### Conclusion
*~280 words · 2 paragraphs*

India's tiger recovery is not complete. Habitats remain fragmented between
reserves, corridors are under pressure from roads and agricultural expansion,
human-wildlife conflict at reserve boundaries imposes real costs on
communities living alongside protected areas, and climate change is beginning
to alter the prey distributions and hydrological cycles that tiger ecosystems
depend on. The 3,682 tigers counted in 2022 are more than double the 2006
baseline — but they inhabit a landscape under sustained and intensifying
pressure. The work of the next sixteen years will be harder than the work
of the last.

What the seven reserves documented here demonstrate, however, is that the
work is possible. In landscapes as different as the Brahmaputra floodplain
and the Aravalli Hills, in states with very different governance capacities
and land use pressures, sustained protection produces measurable recovery.
The monitoring framework that makes this analysis possible — systematic
camera trap surveys, rigorous statistical methods, publicly reported results
— is itself a conservation tool, creating the accountability loop that keeps
management focused and resources allocated. India has built something rare in
global conservation: a large-scale, long-term, evidence-driven recovery
program that works. The tigers in these seven reserves are the evidence.
